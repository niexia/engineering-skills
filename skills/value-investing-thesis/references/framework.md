# Three-lens value investing framework

## How to use this reference

Ask the questions in order. Record evidence, not adjectives. When two lenses
disagree, keep the disagreement visible; that is usually where the risk or the
opportunity lives. Treat every quoted line as a thinking prompt, not as proof.

The three investors overlap, but their emphasis differs:

- **Buffett** asks whether it is a great business at a fair price.
- **Duan Yongping** asks whether it is a real business with a sound model and a
  sound culture, and whether the price is roughly right.
- **Munger** asks what could go wrong, what the incentives are, and whether the
  whole thing is simply too hard.

## Lens 1: Buffett

### Circle of competence

- Can I explain in one paragraph how the company makes money and why customers
  come back?
- What will the business look like in 10 years? What has to be true for that
  picture to hold?
- What do I not know, and can I learn it from primary sources?
- If the stock market closed for 10 years, would I be comfortable owning it?
- Is the demand durable, or is it a fashion, a promotion or a one-off event?

### Business economics

- Revenue growth versus volume, price and mix. Which driver is doing the work?
- Gross margin, operating margin and net margin. Are they structurally higher
  than peers, and why?
- ROIC and ROE versus the cost of capital. Is the business generating cash or
  consuming it?
- Owner earnings = operating cash flow - maintenance capex - working capital
  needed to sustain the business.
- Capital intensity, lease obligations, inventory and receivables. A high-margin
  business can still destroy cash if working capital balloons.
- Customer concentration, supplier concentration, geographic concentration.

### Moat

Test each source separately:

- **Intangible assets**: brand, patent, license, regulatory approval. Can the
  company raise price without losing volume? Has it done so through a downturn?
- **Switching costs**: what does it cost a customer to leave? Is the product
  embedded in a workflow, a habit or a relationship?
- **Network effect**: does each new user make the product more valuable to other
  users? Or is the "network" just social proof that can reverse?
- **Cost advantage**: scale, location, process, sourcing. Is the advantage
  structural or temporary?
- **Efficient scale**: is the market too small to support a second entrant?

Moat verdict:

- **Wide**: survived multiple cycles, pricing power, high returns on capital for
  10+ years, and a clear reason the advantage persists.
- **Narrow**: real but contestable; fashion, technology, regulation or a key
  person can erode it.
- **None**: commodity; returns converge to the cost of capital.

### Management and capital allocation

- Integrity: candor, promises kept, related-party transactions, accounting quality.
- Incentives: compensation, ownership, dilution, insider buying and selling.
- Capital allocation: reinvestment at high ROIC, dividends, buybacks below
  intrinsic value, acquisitions, debt. Ask what the marginal dollar does.
- Succession and key-person risk. A founder-led company can be an advantage and a
  single point of failure.
- "Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome." (Munger)

### Valuation

- Owner earnings yield = normalized owner earnings / enterprise value.
- Reverse DCF: what growth and margin does the current price imply? If the answer
  requires heroic assumptions, the price is not a margin of safety.
- Margin of safety: buy at a discount to a conservatively estimated intrinsic
  value, not at a discount to the last peak price.
- "Price is what you pay; value is what you get."
- "It is far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company
  at a wonderful price."

## Lens 2: Duan Yongping

### 买股票就是买公司

- The stock is a fractional ownership of a business, not a ticker.
- Value is the discounted future cash flows of the business. If you cannot
  estimate them, you do not understand the business well enough.
- Duan often quotes Buffett's test: if you are not willing to own the stock for
  ten years, do not think about owning it for ten minutes.

### 生意模式

- **差异化**: what can the company do that competitors cannot? If the product is
  homogeneous, price competition is inevitable. Duan's rule of thumb: a product
  without differentiation ends up competing on price.
- **定价权**: can it raise prices without losing customers? Does it have pricing
  power in a downturn, or only in a boom?
- **用户需求**: is the demand real, recurring and growing? Or is it a fad, a
  promotion or a channel restock? Talk to users if possible.
- **现金流**: high margin, low capex, negative working capital, low debt. A
  business that needs constant capital to grow is a different business.
- **护城河**: brand, distribution, supply chain, IP, user base. A moat that
  depends on novelty is not a moat.
- **本分**: do the right thing, not the convenient thing; own the consequences.
- **平常心**: no hype, no panic, no chasing; focus on the long term.
- **消费者导向**: solve user needs, not investor narratives.
- **利润之上的追求**: purpose beyond profit; it attracts talent and trust.
- **Stop doing list**: what the company refuses to do is as important as what it
  does. Ask management what they have said no to.
- "做对的事情，把事情做对。"

### 毛估估

- Roughly right is better than precisely wrong.
- Use a range; check the sensitivity to the key assumptions.
- If the valuation only works with heroic assumptions, it does not work.
- Compare the implied return with the opportunity cost, not with cash.

### 机会成本

- Compare with the best alternative, not with the average.
- A mediocre idea at a low price is not a bargain if you have a better idea.
- "敢为天下后，后中争先": a fast follower can win, but only with differentiation
  and better execution.

## Lens 3: Munger

### Inversion

- "Invert, always invert."
- What would make this investment lose 50%? What would make the business
  disappear in 10 years?
- What is the market assuming? Where is the consensus wrong?
- What would a smart short seller say? Steelman the bear case before buying.
- If the thesis requires everything to go right, it is not a thesis; it is a hope.

### Psychology and incentives

- Social proof, scarcity, commitment, envy, overconfidence and recency bias.
- Is demand driven by utility or by a status game? Status goods can be durable
  (luxury) or fragile (fads). Ask which one this is.
- Are management incentives aligned with long-term owners?
- "Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome."

### Too hard

- If you cannot understand the unit economics or the durability of demand, pass.
- There are three ways to lose money: bad business, bad management, bad price.
- Missing a good opportunity is not a sin; losing capital is.
- "It is remarkable how much long-term advantage people like us have gotten by
  trying to be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent."

### Opportunity cost and patience

- "The big money is not in the buying and selling, but in the waiting."
- The 100% buy test: would you buy the whole company at this price and hold it
  for 10 years without a quote?
- A great business at a fair price is rare; do not dilute it with mediocre ideas.

## Consensus, variant perception and what is priced in

A correct description of a great company is not an investment thesis if the price
already reflects it. State:

- **Consensus**: what the market believes about growth, margins, duration and
  risk. Use dated estimates and the current multiple.
- **What is priced in**: the growth and margin implied by the current price
  (reverse DCF or implied multiple).
- **Variant perception**: what you believe differently, why the market may be
  wrong, and what evidence supports it.
- **What would prove you wrong**: the observation that would falsify the variant
  perception.

If you cannot state a variant perception, the correct verdict is WATCH or PASS,
not BUY. The expected return is then beta, not alpha.

**Ticker-swap test**: replace the company with its closest competitor. If the
thesis still reads as plausible, it is generic. Add company-specific evidence or
lower the confidence.

## Stress test and drawdown

The bear case is an operating scenario, not a price target.

- Revenue decline and margin compression: how far, for how long?
- Multiple compression: what multiple does the market pay in a comparable
  downturn?
- Estimate the drawdown from the current price, with the mechanism.
- Identify the early warning and the response.
- Distinguish a temporary drawdown from permanent capital loss.
- If the thesis only works with a low-probability bull case, say so.

## Confidence calibration

| Grade | Meaning | Evidence standard |
|---|---|---|
| A | High confidence | Primary sources, arithmetic checked, key assumptions triangulated, strong bear case tested |
| B | Medium confidence | Mostly primary sources, one or two unresolved estimates, one material uncertainty |
| C | Low confidence | Thin data, multiple estimates, conclusion provisional; size accordingly |

Grade the conclusion and the two or three assumptions that do the most work, not
every sentence. Carry the overall grade into `summary.json`.

## Scoring rubric

Score each dimension 1-5. Use evidence, not gut feel.

| Score | Business quality | Moat | Management | Valuation | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | High ROIC, recurring demand, strong cash conversion | Wide, tested through cycles | Integrity, owner mindset, superior allocation | Deep discount to intrinsic value | Low leverage, low concentration, predictable |
| 4 | Good economics, some cyclicality | Narrow but improving | Competent, aligned | Modest discount | Manageable risks |
| 3 | Average economics | Narrow, contestable | Adequate | Fair | Normal risks |
| 2 | Low returns, capital intensive | Eroding | Governance concerns | Expensive | High leverage or concentration |
| 1 | Value-destroying | None | Untrustworthy | Speculative | Existential risk |

Verdict rules:

- **BUY** requires business >= 4, moat >= 3, management >= 4, valuation >= 4 and
  risk >= 3.
- **WATCH** if business >= 4 but valuation <= 3, or a key uncertainty is unresolved.
- **PASS** if business <= 2, moat <= 2, management <= 2, or there is an accounting
  or governance red flag.

These are guidelines, not a mechanical formula. Explain any override.

## Red flags

- **Accounting**: revenue recognition, receivables growing faster than revenue,
  inventory growing faster than revenue, capitalized costs, related-party
  transactions, frequent adjustments, auditor changes.
- **Governance**: controlling-shareholder conflicts, share pledges, dilution,
  buybacks at high prices, insider selling, opaque compensation.
- **Operating**: guidance cuts, channel stuffing, price cuts, store closures, key
  customer or IP loss, lawsuits, regulatory action.
- **Narrative**: "new era", "platform", "ecosystem" without unit economics.
  Compare with the last hot story in the sector and how it ended.

## Normalized earnings bridge

Start with reported net profit. Adjust:

- one-off gains and losses;
- FX gains and losses;
- stock-based compensation, if material;
- inventory write-downs and restructuring;
- excess margin from a temporary boom;
- maintenance capex and working-capital normalization.

Result: normalized owner earnings. Show a low, base and high estimate. Explain
each adjustment and label it as reported, adjusted or estimated.

## Scenario valuation

For each case:

- Revenue = volume × price × mix, or a segment build.
- Margin = gross margin - operating expense ratio.
- Net profit = operating profit - tax - minorities.
- Multiple = what a rational buyer would pay for this growth, ROIC and moat.
- Equity value = net profit × multiple + net cash - debt - other liabilities.
- Per-share value = equity value / diluted shares.
- Return = per-share value / current price - 1.

Expected value = sum of probability × return. State that the probabilities are
subjective and show how the answer changes if they are wrong.

## Decision and position sizing

- **Core position (5-10%)**: high confidence, wide moat, margin of safety.
- **Satellite (2-5%)**: good business, some uncertainty.
- **Option (0-2%)**: early-stage or turnaround; size for total loss.
- No leverage, no shorting, no concentrated bets without deep research.
- Define sell rules before buying: thesis broken, valuation extreme, better
  opportunity, or permanent capital-loss risk.

## Pre-mortem template

It is 10 years from now and the investment lost 70%. Write the headline. What
happened? Rank the causes by probability and impact. For each cause, define an
early-warning indicator and a response.

## Tracking checklist

- Revenue growth by segment and geography.
- Unit economics: store productivity, online conversion, customer acquisition cost.
- Moat evidence: pricing, market share, retention, repeat purchase.
- Balance sheet: inventory days, receivables days, net cash, debt.
- Capital allocation: buyback price versus intrinsic value, dividends, M&A.
- Management: guidance credibility, insider transactions, key hires and departures.
- Valuation: implied expectations versus actual delivery.
